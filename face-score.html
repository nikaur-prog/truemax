How attractive am I? What a face score can tell you

Updated 20 September 2026 · Published by TrueMax

No app can settle how attractive you are to everyone. A face score can organise what one photograph shows: proportions, angles and relationships between features. Its value is in the explanation, not the ranking alone.

What is a face score?

A face score converts a set of visible measurements into a common scale. Those measurements can include facial thirds, jaw angle, eye spacing, symmetry and the relationship between features. A useful report says which measurements were used, what the reference represents and when photo quality limits the reading. The facial measurement guide explains these terms individually.

TrueMax reports its overall result on a 0 to 10 scale and keeps population percentile separate. The score describes agreement with the app's reference ranges. The percentile describes where that result sits in the selected reference population. They are related, but they are not interchangeable.

How do face rating apps work?

  1. Find facial landmarks. A model locates points around the eyes, nose, lips, jaw and other visible structures.
  2. Normalise the photograph. The app accounts for image size and uses stable facial distances so a larger photo does not create larger measurements.
  3. Calculate proportions and angles. Landmark positions become interpretable facial measurements.
  4. Compare with reference data. Each usable measurement is compared with the relevant reference range.
  5. Report the result and its limits. Weak or unavailable measurements should be marked rather than quietly treated as certain.

A worked example: a ratio is not a rating

Imagine an illustrative front photo where the visible nose width spans 40 pixels and the mouth spans 64 pixels. The nose-to-mouth ratio is 40 ÷ 64 = 0.625. Double the photo size and it becomes 80 ÷ 128: still 0.625. That is why a ratio is more useful than an unscaled pixel distance.

The number does not mean “6.25 out of 10.” A model still needs a stated reference and a scoring rule to interpret it. Another app might report mouth-to-nose instead, which is 64 ÷ 40 = 1.6. Those two measurements describe the same example in opposite directions, not a disagreement about the face. These figures demonstrate arithmetic, not an ideal proportion.

Why can the same face receive different scores?

A photograph is not the face itself. A phone held too close can enlarge the centre of the face. A raised or lowered camera changes apparent proportions. Expression moves the eyes, lips and jaw. Hard side lighting can make symmetry look different. Filters can alter the input before analysis begins.

For a more repeatable result, use even front lighting, a neutral expression, a level camera and enough distance to avoid wide-angle distortion. Remove glasses and keep hair away from the facial outline. When tracking change, use the same camera position and lighting each time.

Two apps can also use different endpoints, reference ranges, exclusions or score scales. First compare the raw value and unit, then the point placement, and only then the score. An eyebrow angle is not canthal tilt; a front jaw outline is not the side-profile gonial angle. Matching names is not enough to establish matching measurements.

Can an app tell me how attractive I am?

It can describe measurable geometry and compare it with a stated reference. It cannot measure chemistry, movement, voice, expression, style, confidence or individual preference. Those are major parts of real-world attraction. No face score is a universal measure of a person's attractiveness.

How to use the result well

Look for patterns rather than fixating on the total. Separate structural measurements from changeable presentation. Hair, facial hair, skin care, body composition, sleep, posture, expression and photography can affect appearance without pretending bone structure changed overnight.

Start with the highest-impact change that is safe, affordable and reversible. Recheck under comparable conditions. If a concern involves skin disease, dental health, breathing, pain or body-image distress, an app is not the right professional. Speak with a qualified clinician.
