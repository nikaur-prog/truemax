Practical appearance guide

How to become more attractive without guessing

Updated 20 September 2026 · Published by TrueMax

If you want to look better, begin with the impression you want to create, not a list of facial flaws. Hair, grooming, clothing fit and photographs give you practical choices. Pick a change you can afford, maintain and reverse if it does not suit you.

Begin with a consistent baseline

Before changing anything, take a clear front and side photograph in neutral lighting. Keep the camera level and avoid portrait filters. Write down what you want to improve in ordinary language, such as looking more rested, making hair frame the face better or creating a more consistent skin-care routine.

A facial analysis can organise visible proportions, but it should not replace your own priorities. A technically unusual measurement may already be a feature you like. The goal is not to erase individuality.

Turn “I want to look better” into one decision

A useful goal names the context. “I want a haircut that still looks tidy before my next appointment” gives a stylist something to work with. “I need a higher face score” does not tell you what to buy, change or practise.

  1. Name the situation. Everyday appearance, work, dating photos or a specific event.
  2. Set a limit. Choose the money and weekly maintenance time you are comfortable spending.
  3. Choose one reversible test. Try a different hair part, a cleaner facial-hair outline, an outfit adjustment or a new photo setup.
  4. Decide how to review it. Ask whether it suits you and is easy to maintain, not whether every viewer would rank it higher.

A useful order of operations

  1. Keep the routine realistic. Choose grooming tasks that fit the time you actually have. A plan that needs an hour every morning is not useful if you have ten minutes.
  2. Hair and facial hair. Shape, length and maintenance change the frame around the face. Bring reference photos to a skilled barber or stylist and ask what works with your hair texture.
  3. Organise skin care before buying more. List what you already use and follow its instructions. Persistent irritation, acne or other skin concerns need individual advice, not a routine inferred from a face score.
  4. Teeth and oral health. Regular dental care matters more than extreme whitening. Pain, bite concerns and gum problems belong with a qualified dentist.
  5. Posture and presentation. Clothes that fit, clean shoes, well-kept brows and a relaxed upright posture can change the whole impression without changing the face.
  6. Keep appearance goals separate from health decisions. Do not use a photo score to set a target weight or decide on a treatment. Health and nutrition questions belong with a qualified professional.

Choose one high-impact change at a time

When several things change together, you cannot tell what helped. Pick one change and define what completion looks like. For a haircut, that might mean keeping two reference photos, agreeing a shape with your stylist and checking whether you can style it yourself a week later. Use comparable photographs rather than checking the mirror repeatedly throughout the day.

Keep changes proportionate to the evidence. If a result depends heavily on one poor photograph or an app labels a measurement uncertain, do not spend money trying to fix it.

An example that does not need a shopping list

For a clearer profile photo, first remove the filter and match the camera height and lighting. Then compare two hair or facial-hair styles you can already create. You have tested presentation without assuming that your jaw structure needs changing or buying a product to move a score.

Separate facial structure from visible definition

A jaw angle describes geometry between placed points. It is not a direct measurement of how sharp the jaw looks, and it does not measure body-fat percentage. Lighting, pose, facial hair and the visible outline are separate considerations. The side-profile guide explains what a photograph can and cannot establish.

When professional help makes sense

Use a dermatologist for persistent skin concerns, a dentist or orthodontist for oral health and bite questions, and a registered health professional for nutrition or body-image concerns. Permanent procedures require an independent clinical assessment, realistic expectations and time away from social-media pressure. An appearance app cannot assess medical suitability.

Measure progress without becoming consumed by it

Review monthly, not hourly. Use the same light, distance and camera. Track a few behaviours you control, such as sleep consistency or completing a grooming routine. Stop if the process is increasing distress, compulsive checking or social withdrawal. Looking better should support your life, not take it over.
